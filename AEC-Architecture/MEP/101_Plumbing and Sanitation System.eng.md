# Plumbing and Sanitation System

## 1. Water Supply System 💧

**What is a Water Supply System?**

A system that draws water from the municipal water supply and delivers it to each floor and unit of a building.

There are three main methods:

- **Direct Connection** — A method that uses the pressure from the water main directly to supply water.
The building's plumbing is connected directly to the municipal water supply without a separate tank.
→ Suitable for low-rise buildings (3–5 stories or less) and is hygienic.
- **Elevated Tank (Gravity Tank)** — A method where a water tank is installed on the building's rooftop, and water is sent down by gravity.
→ Provides consistent water pressure, and water remaining in the tank can still be used during supply interruptions.
This method has long been used in mid- to high-rise apartment buildings.
- **Pressure Tank** — A method that uses air pressure inside a sealed tank to push water out.
→ No rooftop tank is needed, resulting in a cleaner building appearance. Commonly used in small to medium-sized buildings.

> 🔎 The taller a building is, the more problems arise — water pressure becomes too high on lower floors and too low on upper floors. To solve this, a **Zoning** technique is used, dividing the building into sections by floor to regulate water pressure.

## 2. Hot Water Supply System 🔥

**What is a Hot Water Supply System?**

A system that supplies hot water to each space in a building.
Unlike cold water piping, the water temperature must be maintained throughout delivery, making the design more complex.

---

**📍 Hot Water Supply Type 1 — Individual vs Central**

- **Individual System** — A method where each unit or space has its own boiler or water heater installed separately.
→ Since it only operates when needed, energy waste is minimal.
Suitable for individual homes and small buildings.
- **Central System** — A method where the entire building shares a single large heat source (boiler room).
→ Advantageous for places like large hotels, offices, and hospitals that need large volumes of hot water 24 hours a day.
Management is convenient, but heat loss increases as piping gets longer.

---

**📍 Hot Water Supply Type 2 — Instantaneous vs Storage**

- **Instantaneous (Tankless)** — A method that rapidly heats water at the moment it is needed and supplies it immediately.
→ Takes up less space since there is no separate storage tank.
Hot water may run short if demand suddenly spikes.
- **Storage Tank** — A method that pre-heats and stores hot water in a large-capacity tank.
→ Large amounts of hot water can be used at once.
The downside is that energy must be continuously used to keep the stored water from cooling.
Suitable for places with high hot water usage and peak hours, such as hotels and bathhouses.

---

**📍 Boiler Systems**

- Handles both heating (underfloor heating, radiators) and hot water supply simultaneously.
- **Condensing Boiler** — A boiler that improves efficiency by recovering heat from the hot exhaust gases that would otherwise be wasted.
Reduces fuel costs by approximately 10–15% compared to conventional boilers.
- Air bleeding (removing air from pipes) must be performed periodically to maintain heating efficiency.
→ If air accumulates in the pipes, hot water flow is blocked, causing certain areas to remain cold.

---

**📍 Eco-Friendly Hot Water Methods — Emerging Trends**

In addition to gas boilers, eco-friendly hot water methods that save energy are becoming increasingly popular.

- **Solar Water Heating** — A method that uses solar collectors installed on the roof to capture heat from sunlight and warm the water.
→ Fuel costs are virtually zero. On cloudy days or at night, it is used together with a supplementary heat source (boiler).
Widely adopted in single-family homes, guesthouses, and public buildings.
- **Heat Pump Water Heating** — Working on the reverse principle of an air conditioner, this method extracts heat from outdoor air or groundwater to heat water.
→ Although it uses electricity, it can produce **2–4 times more thermal energy** than the electricity consumed, making it highly efficient.
→ With low carbon emissions, it is becoming a core system in green buildings and zero-energy buildings.

> 🔎 The government mandates that new buildings incorporate a certain percentage of renewable energy systems (solar thermal, heat pumps, etc.), so these technologies are expected to become increasingly widespread.

## 3. Drainage and Ventilation System 🚿

**What is a Drainage System?**

A system that carries used water and sewage out of a building to the external sewer lines.

---

**📍 Trap**

- A **Trap** is installed to prevent sewer odors from flowing back into the building.
<img src="./101-102_img/101-1.png" width="66%">

🔎 **What is a Trap?** It is the U-shaped bend in the pipe beneath sinks and toilets.
Water always remains pooled in this U-bend, acting as a barrier to block foul odors and pests from rising through the sewer pipes.
This is also why odors can come up if water hasn't been used for a long time — the trap water evaporates!

- Vent pipes are installed together to ensure smooth drainage flow.

---

**📍 Types of Drainage — Sewage, Gray Water, and Stormwater**

Buildings produce more than one type of wastewater. Separate piping systems are used based on the nature of the water for proper treatment.

- **Sewage** — Water from toilets (mixed with human waste).
Due to its high contamination level, it must be sent to a sewage treatment plant.
- **Gray Water** — Domestic wastewater from sinks, bathtubs, and kitchen sinks.
Less contaminated than sewage, but still discharged through sewer lines.
- **Stormwater** — Rainwater that falls on roofs and outdoor surfaces.
With low contamination, it can be collected through separate pipes and either discharged into rivers or **recycled** for landscaping and toilet flushing.

> 🔎 If sewage and rainwater are mixed in the same pipes, sewage treatment plants become overloaded during heavy rain. That's why modern buildings use **separate drainage systems** to strictly separate sewage/gray water from stormwater.

## 4. Sanitary Fixtures 🚽

**What are Sanitary Fixtures?**

This is a collective term for fixtures that directly handle water (sinks, toilets, bathtubs, etc.) and their associated equipment.
Sanitary fixtures are the final connection points where the water supply, hot water supply, and drainage piping all converge.

---

**📍 Types of Sanitary Fixtures**

- **Toilet (Water Closet)** — Classified by flushing method: flush valve type (public restrooms) and tank type (residential homes).
Water-saving toilets (6L or less per flush) are increasingly becoming mandatory.

> 💡 **Key Point:** Water-saving toilets are **eco-friendly products designed to flush waste with 6L or less of water, compared to approximately 12–13L for conventional toilets**.

- **Urinal** — Primarily installed in men's restrooms of public and commercial buildings.
Sensor-based automatic flushing has become standard.
- **Washbasin / Lavatory** — Used for handwashing and face washing.
Various installation types are available, including wall-mounted, pedestal, and under-counter styles.
- **Bathtub / Shower** — Installed in residential and accommodation facilities.
Bathtubs are installed with drainage traps to prevent sewer odors from backing up.
- **Kitchen Sink** — A water supply and drainage fixture in cooking areas.
Caution is needed to prevent drain pipe clogging from food debris.

> 🔎 Sanitary fixtures are not merely interior design elements. They must be designed in conjunction with plumbing plans, including water pressure, drainage slope, and trap placement, to function properly.


> 💡 **Key Point:** Sanitary fixtures are the point where all three systems — water supply, drainage, and ventilation — connect. If any one of them is poorly designed, problems such as odors, backflow, and clogging will occur.

## 5. Sewage Treatment Facilities ♻️

**What are Sewage Treatment Facilities?**

These are on-site facilities that treat and discharge building sewage (human waste and domestic wastewater) in areas not connected to the public sewer system.
Urban buildings connect to public sewers, but rural, mountainous, and remote areas require separate treatment facilities.

---

**📍 Sewage Treatment Methods**

- **Septic Tank** — A method that collects sewage in a tank and decomposes/settles it using microorganisms.
Simple in structure and low in cost, but offers limited treatment efficiency and is considered an outdated method.
<img src="./101-102_img/101-4.png" width="66%">

- **Advanced Treatment Facility** — A method that purifies sewage through biological treatment processes (aeration tank, sedimentation tank, disinfection tank).
The discharged water must meet effluent standards (BOD, SS, etc.).
→ Installed in buildings not connected to the sewer system, such as guesthouses, resorts, and small factories.

> 🔎 **BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)** refers to the amount of oxygen required to decompose organic matter in water. The lower the value, the cleaner the water.

> 💡 **Key Point:** Discharging sewage without treatment leads to water pollution and legal penalties. Regular cleaning of treatment facilities is essential.
